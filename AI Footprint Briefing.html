A staff briefing on AI & energy

How much does AI actually cost the planet?

Some of your colleagues are excited to bring AI into their classrooms. Others have stopped using it over what they've heard about its energy and water use. Both are working from real numbers — usually not the same ones. This briefing lays out what the underlying research actually says, where the two camps talk past each other, and where the real disagreement is.

01

The one number nearly everyone agrees on

Independent measurements from Google's own published methodology, the AI research group Epoch AI, and MIT Technology Review's 2025 investigation all land in the same range for a typical text prompt to a chatbot like ChatGPT or Gemini:

0.24–0.4 watt-hours per prompt

Google's own published figure for a median Gemini text prompt, 0.24 Wh, is the closest thing to a company-verified number in this whole debate. Google describes it as roughly the energy of watching TV for nine seconds. A 2026 report from the United Nations University puts a working average slightly higher, at 0.42 Wh, because it blends in longer and more complex prompts — but even that stays in the same order of magnitude.

The more careful recent numbers put ordinary text search and an ordinary AI text query in roughly the same place.

02

Myth vs. reality, from both directions

Pick the perspective closer to your own. Same underlying research either way — the claims people repeat just tend to run in opposite directions.

Claim
"A single AI prompt is basically free — it's not worth worrying about."
Reality Mostly true for a text prompt in isolation: 0.3 Wh is about 0.0005% of an average American's daily emissions, per climate writer Andy Masley's math. But that math describes one prompt, not what happens when a school of 500 students each sends dozens of prompts a day, every school day, for the rest of the year. Small numbers still accumulate.
Claim
"AI will make the grid greener on net — it's a climate solution, not a climate problem."
Reality The International Energy Agency does project that AI-optimized industry and grids could cut more emissions than AI's own electricity use adds, by 2035. That's a real, cited projection — and also a projection, resting on assumptions about how AI gets deployed. It doesn't cancel out the data centers being built and powered by gas and coal right now.
Claim
"Efficiency is improving so fast the footprint is basically shrinking."
Reality Per-query efficiency really is improving fast — Google reported its median Gemini prompt used 33× less energy in May 2025 than a year earlier. But total AI electricity demand is still projected to climb, because usage is growing faster than efficiency gains can offset it. Economists call this the rebound effect, or Jevons Paradox: cheaper computing invites more computing.
Claim
"Every ChatGPT query is a small climate disaster."
Reality A single text prompt costs roughly what a microwave uses in one second. You'd have to send about 2,000 prompts in a day to raise your personal emissions by 1%. The individual-prompt framing that circulates online overstates the per-use case by a wide margin — the real story is elsewhere (see below).
Claim
"Training one model creates hundreds of thousands of kilograms of CO₂."
Reality This traces to a widely repeated 2019 Strubell et al. figure that's frequently misapplied — the original number described a much larger neural-architecture-search process across many models, not one single training run. The real number for training a single frontier model is still large: the UN University's 2026 report estimates roughly 25,000–42,000 tonnes CO₂e to train a model on the scale of GPT-4 or GPT-5. That's genuinely big — just a different (and better-sourced) number than the one that usually gets quoted.
Claim
"AI data centers are draining water supplies and burning coal, full stop."
Reality This is true in specific, documented places — not everywhere uniformly. Microsoft's Netherlands data center used 84 million liters of water in a drought year, far above its own earlier estimate. Ireland's data centers now use more electricity than all of its urban households combined, prompting a construction pause around Dublin. The impact is real but concentrated and site-specific — which is exactly why where a data center gets built matters as much as how big it is.

06

Cutting the footprint down

None of this argues for using AI tools less thoughtfully — it argues for using them more deliberately. In order of how much difference each one actually makes:

1

Ask whether you need AI for this task at all

The biggest lever isn't a setting, it's the decision before you open the tool. A quick fact lookup, a calculation, or a question you could answer from a textbook doesn't need a language model — and skipping it is the one habit that reduces AI energy use to zero for that task, not just by a percentage.

2

Default to text, and treat image and video generation as a deliberate choice

This is the single highest-leverage setting, because the gap between tasks is 10–1,000×, not a few percent. Save image generation for when a real image is the point of the assignment, and treat video generation, which can cost as much as an hour of streaming per clip, as an occasional tool rather than a default output format.

3

Use the smallest model that actually does the job

Most chatbot platforms let you pick a lighter or faster model (sometimes labeled "mini," "flash," "instant," or similar) alongside their flagship one. Lighter models can use an order of magnitude less energy per query, and for routine tasks — drafting an email, summarizing a short passage — they're often just as good.

4

Write a better prompt instead of sending five worse ones

Every follow-up ("no, make it shorter," "try again") is a full additional query. A little upfront thought about what you actually want, especially for longer or more complex requests, usually costs less energy than iterating your way there.

5

Turn off AI features you're not actively using

Auto-summarize, always-on writing suggestions, and AI search overviews all run whether or not you asked for that specific result. Most can be disabled in settings, which removes the cost of queries you never chose to make.

None of these require becoming an expert, and none require giving up AI tools. They're the same category of habit as turning off lights in an empty room — not because any one instance matters much, but because the habit is what scales.

07

What's actually worth watching

Ranked from the smallest lever to the biggest one. If you only have energy to think about one or two of these, start from the bottom.

1

Which task, not just how many prompts

The gap between a short text answer and a generated video isn't 2× or 5× — it's 200–1,000×. A classroom norm of "text first, image or video only when it adds real value" does more than any amount of prompt-counting.

2

The rebound effect

Every efficiency gain in this space has historically been followed by more total use, not less. That means "the new model is more efficient" is not, on its own, a reason to expect AI's total footprint to shrink.

3

Where the data center sits

The same electricity has wildly different water and land costs depending on the grid it comes from. A data center on a hydro-heavy grid can have a tiny carbon footprint and a large water footprint, or the reverse on a coal-heavy grid. There is no location that's simply "clean."

4

Almost no company discloses real numbers

OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic all treat per-query energy use as a trade secret. Every figure in this briefing, including the companies' own occasional disclosures, is a third-party estimate or a partial release. That opacity is arguably the biggest single problem — it's hard to govern what isn't measured.

5

Who gets the infrastructure and who doesn't

Only 32 countries, about 16% of them, host AI-specialized data centers at all, and 90% of that capacity sits in just the US and China. The environmental cost and the economic benefit of AI infrastructure don't land on the same communities. For a global classroom conversation, this is often the more consequential inequity than the energy math itself.

08

Questions for a staff meeting

  1. 01If a single prompt costs almost nothing but our building's AI use adds up to something real, where's the right place to set a norm — the individual prompt, or the tool defaults we choose as a school?
  2. 02Does it change anything to know that image and video generation cost 10–1,000× more than text? Should that shape which AI tools we adopt for which assignments?
  3. 03We ask students to cite sources carefully. Should we hold "AI uses 10× the energy of a search" and similarly popular claims to the same standard before repeating them in class?
  4. 04If neither "AI is basically free" nor "AI is a climate disaster" survives close reading, what's the version of this conversation we actually want students having?

International Energy Agency, Energy and AI (2025)strong
The most authoritative single document on this topic. An intergovernmental agency's own modeling of data-center demand through 2035, including the finding that AI's own emissions and AI-enabled efficiency gains elsewhere are both real, and neither cancels the other out.
IEA, "The carbon footprint of streaming video: fact-checking the headlines" (2020)strong
The source of the streaming-video figure in the comparison chart, and a genuinely useful precedent: a viral "30 minutes of Netflix = 4 miles of driving" claim that turned out to be overstated by roughly 90× once someone checked the underlying assumptions. The AI energy debate is arguably having the same fight right now.

The primary source for the 0.24 Wh Gemini figure used throughout this briefing, with a published technical methodology. It's also the company measuring its own homework, so treat the efficiency-gain claims as self-reported.
