A staff briefing on AI & energy

How much does AI actually cost the planet?

Some of your colleagues are excited to bring AI into their classrooms. Others have real concerns about its energy and water use. This briefing tries to separate four questions that usually get collapsed into one: how much energy a single task uses, how large AI's aggregate footprint is, what burden falls on the specific communities hosting that infrastructure, and whether a given classroom use is worth its cost. The numbers below are best current estimates, not settled facts — and several of them are corrections to an earlier draft of this same page.

Last reviewed October 1, 2026 — first against an independent accuracy review that corrected a number of overstated or mislabeled figures in the first draft, then again against a U.S. GAO technology assessment that added the hardware-manufacturing and national-scale figures in "What's actually worth watching." See Sources for specifics.

01

Where the recent estimates land

A handful of separately run 2025 estimates for a short, ordinary chatbot text response — a quick question, not a long document or a multi-step agent task — land in a similar order of magnitude. That's useful context. It is not a verified industry consensus: each number describes a different model, on a different date, measured a different way, and none of it covers heavier workloads like reasoning or agentic tasks (more on that below).

0.03–0.4 Whseveral separate 2025 estimates for a short text response

Google's disclosed figure — 0.24 Wh for the median Gemini Apps text prompt in May 2025 — is the only one of these based on the company's own internal measurement rather than outside modeling. Google's own write-up is explicit that the data hasn't been independently verified and doesn't describe every prompt or future performance. Epoch AI's roughly 0.3 Wh is a modeled estimate for GPT-4o under stated assumptions, not a direct measurement. The UN University's 2026 report uses 0.42 Wh as a representative planning figure for its own calculations. Treat these as three separate estimates with a name, a date, and a method attached — not one settled number.

That first row is worth sitting with. The familiar claim that an AI query burns 10× the energy of a web search traces to an identifiable 2023 estimate (about 2.9 Wh, modeled from a SemiAnalysis assumption about ChatGPT's early infrastructure) compared against Google's 0.3 Wh search figure — which dates to 2009. Comparing a 2023 estimate for one system against a 16-year-old figure for a completely different, long-since-rebuilt one doesn't reliably establish a multiplier in either direction. Nobody has published a rigorous, current, apples-to-apples comparison of a search and an AI query. That's worth noticing the next time someone states one with confidence — including an earlier draft of this page, which claimed the opposite overstatement.

02

Myth vs. reality, from both directions

Pick the perspective closer to your own. These aren't two settled positions where one side has already won — they're claims worth checking against what the underlying research actually supports.

Claim
"A single AI prompt is basically free — it's not worth worrying about."
Reality Roughly true for an ordinary text prompt in isolation: at around 0.3 Wh, that's about 0.0005% of an average American's daily emissions, per climate writer Andy Masley's math. But that describes one prompt, not what happens when a school of 500 students each sends dozens of prompts a day, every school day, all year — and it doesn't describe a longer, reasoning-heavy, or agentic request, which can use far more. Small numbers still accumulate, and not every AI task is small.
Claim
"AI will make the grid greener on net — it's a climate solution, not a climate problem."
Reality The International Energy Agency does project that AI-enabled efficiency elsewhere in the economy could exceed the emissions from data centers' own electricity use, by 2035. That's a real, cited projection — and also a projection, resting on assumptions about how AI actually gets deployed and adopted. It doesn't cancel out the data centers being built and powered by gas and coal right now, and IEA's own 2026 update is explicit that this is a possibility, not a guarantee.
Claim
"Efficiency is improving so fast the footprint is basically shrinking."
Reality Per-query efficiency really is improving fast — Google reported its median Gemini prompt used 33× less energy in May 2025 than a year earlier. But total AI electricity demand is still projected to climb, because falling costs tend to invite more use, a dynamic economists call the rebound effect. That's a documented tendency, not an iron law — but it does mean a given efficiency gain is not, by itself, a reliable reason to expect AI's total footprint to shrink.
Claim
"Every ChatGPT query is a small climate disaster."
Reality An ordinary short text prompt costs roughly what a microwave uses in a second or two. You'd need something like 2,000 such prompts in a day to raise your personal emissions by 1%, by Andy Masley's math. The individual-prompt framing that circulates online overstates the case for an ordinary text query by a wide margin — though it understates the case for image or video generation, and for reasoning or agentic tasks, which can use one to three orders of magnitude more (see the chart below).
Claim
"Training one model creates hundreds of thousands of kilograms of CO₂."
Reality The widely repeated 2019 Strubell et al. figure this traces to actually described a neural-architecture-search process across many models, not one training run — so the citation is commonly misapplied. But don't read that as reassurance: the UN University's 2026 report estimates GPT-4-scale training at roughly 25,000 tonnes CO₂e (25 million kilograms), and a GPT-5-scale model at roughly 42,000 tonnes — both far larger than the misattributed figure, not smaller. That GPT-5 number is explicitly a scaling projection, since no real GPT-5 training-energy data has been published. The correction here isn't "it's not that bad." It's "the commonly cited source was wrong, and the real number for a frontier model is worse."
Claim
"AI data centers are draining water supplies and burning coal, full stop."
Reality True in specific, documented places — not everywhere uniformly, and not always for the reason assumed. Microsoft's Netherlands data center used 84 million liters of water in 2021, a drought year, far above its own earlier estimate; contemporaneous reporting notes that construction activity, not just operations, contributed to that total. In Ireland, data centers of all kinds accounted for about 23% of the country's metered electricity in 2025, versus about 18% for all urban households combined, per Ireland's Central Statistics Office — a real and striking comparison, though it covers all data centers, not AI-specific ones. Ireland's grid regulator adopted a stricter new connection policy for data centers in December 2025, rather than a blanket construction ban. The impact is real but concentrated and site-specific, which is exactly why where infrastructure gets built matters as much as how big it is.

Streaming video had this exact argument a few years ago. A viral 2019 claim that thirty minutes of Netflix emits as much as driving four miles turned out, once the IEA checked the underlying assumptions, to be overstated by roughly 90×. The corrected number, 36 g CO₂e per hour, is what's plotted above. The same skepticism is worth aiming at every figure on this page, including the ones that favor a reassuring conclusion.

The pattern that survives the caveats: an ordinary search, chatbot reply, or AI image sits down near hundredths to a few grams of CO₂e; even a full hour of streaming or a heavier AI video sits in the tens of grams. Driving, flying, and eating meat start roughly where that cluster ends and run one to two orders of magnitude higher. None of these are measured on quite the same terms — see the table above — but the gap between "ordinary digital task" and "physical, biological thing" is wide enough to survive that imprecision.

Be careful what that gap does and doesn't settle. It's a statement about per-task magnitude, nothing more. It says nothing about whether a specific data center is straining a specific community's water supply, whether a specific grid can absorb the new demand without building more fossil generation, or whether a specific classroom's use of the tool was worth it — the local-burden and educational-value questions from the top of this page. Scale doesn't answer those, and a chart that makes AI look small can be used to wave them away. It also doesn't run in reverse: a real, large footprint across a company's entire operation doesn't mean any single ordinary text prompt was wasteful. Both can be true at once, which is exactly why this page keeps the four questions separate instead of averaging them into one verdict.

06

Cutting the footprint down

None of this argues for using AI tools less thoughtfully — it argues for using them more deliberately. Roughly in order of how much difference each one makes:

1

Ask whether you need AI for this task at all

The biggest lever isn't a setting, it's the decision before you open the tool. A quick fact lookup, a calculation, or a question you could answer from a textbook doesn't need a language model — and skipping it removes that task's AI energy use entirely, not just reduces it.

2

Default to text, and treat image and video generation as a deliberate choice

This is the highest-leverage setting, because the gap between task types spans one to three orders of magnitude, not a few percent. Save image generation for when a real image is the point of the assignment, and treat video generation — which can cost as much as an hour of streaming per clip, sometimes far more — as an occasional tool rather than a default output format.

3

Use the lightest model that actually does the job

Most chatbot platforms offer a lighter or faster option (sometimes labeled "mini," "flash," "instant," or similar) alongside their flagship model. These generally use less energy per query, and for routine tasks — drafting an email, summarizing a short passage — they're often good enough. No independent, cross-provider ranking of exactly how much lighter models save currently exists, so treat "generally less" as the honest claim rather than a specific multiplier.

4

Think before you iterate

Every follow-up message is a separate query, and long conversations mean some of the earlier exchange gets reprocessed each time too, though exactly how much depends on the platform's caching, which isn't public information. A little upfront thought about what you actually want usually beats sending five rough attempts — though not by a fixed or guaranteed amount.

5

Turn off the AI features you can actually turn off

Auto-summarize and always-on writing suggestions can usually be disabled in settings, which does stop that specific computation. Search AI overviews are a partial exception: Google's own help documentation says AI Overviews can't be fully disabled as a core Search feature, and its "Web" filter changes what's displayed without necessarily stopping the underlying computation. Check the specific product's documentation rather than assuming a toggle exists.

None of these require becoming an expert, and none require giving up AI tools. They're the same category of habit as turning off lights in an empty room — not because any one instance matters much, but because the habit is what scales.

07

What's actually worth watching

Roughly ordered from the smallest lever to the biggest one. If you only have energy to think about one or two, start from the bottom.

1

Which task, not just how many prompts

The gap between a short text answer and a generated video isn't 2× or 5× — using this page's own figures, it runs from roughly 200× to well over a thousand times. A classroom norm of "text first, image or video only when it adds real value" does more than any amount of prompt-counting.

2

The rebound effect

Efficiency gains lower the cost of each query, which tends to encourage more use — that's the logic behind the rebound effect, or Jevons Paradox. It doesn't automatically follow that any specific improvement will increase total use, but it does mean "the new model is more efficient" is not, on its own, a reliable reason to expect AI's total footprint to shrink.

3

Where the data center sits

The same electricity has different water and land costs depending on the grid it comes from. A data center on a hydro-heavy grid can pair a tiny carbon footprint with a large water footprint; a coal-heavy grid often raises the carbon side without necessarily lowering the water side, since thermal power plants use substantial cooling water too. There's no location that's simply "clean" on every dimension at once.

4

Disclosure is incomplete, not absent

OpenAI and Anthropic have never published a per-query energy or carbon figure. Google and Mistral both have, for specific workloads — Google for a median Gemini text prompt, Mistral for one 400-token response. That's real progress, but the two use different accounting methods, cover different tasks, and can't yet be meaningfully compared to each other. Incomplete and inconsistent disclosure, not total silence, is the more accurate complaint — and it's still enough to make independent verification hard.

5

Who gets the infrastructure and who doesn't

As of the UN University's 2025 data, only 32 countries host AI-specialized data centers at all, and about 90% of that capacity sits in the US and China. The environmental cost and the economic benefit of AI infrastructure don't land on the same communities. For a global classroom conversation, this is often the more consequential inequity than the energy math itself.

6

The real scale: how big is this, nationally?

Everything above is about a single task. Zoomed out: U.S. data centers were about 4% of U.S. electricity demand in 2022, and the International Energy Agency projected 6% by 2026. The U.S. Government Accountability Office's 2025 technology assessment cites separate estimates putting data centers at 6.7–12% of U.S. electricity by 2028, with AI applications specifically responsible for something like 10–20% of data center electricity today — a share one financial-industry estimate expects to reach 20% by 2030. GAO is also explicit that "it is unclear what portion of data center electricity consumption is related to generative AI" specifically, as opposed to other computing — the same measurement gap this page keeps running into, confirmed by a government audit. These are national, aggregate numbers: a single classroom's use is a vanishingly small fraction of them, and the aggregate number answers a policy question, not whether any one prompt was worth sending.

7

Manufacturing the hardware may outweigh running it

Nearly every figure on this page, including its own comparison chart, counts only the electricity used to train or run a model. GAO's 2025 report cites an analysis finding that embodied carbon from manufacturing the computing hardware — mining raw materials, fabricating chips, building and shipping servers — adds roughly 50% on top of the emissions from training and using AI. Hardware also has a finite life: GPUs are generally considered obsolete after about 4 years, and what happens to them afterward is a lifecycle stage with almost no public data behind it. If that 50% figure is in the right range, every operational number on this page is undercounting the true footprint by closer to half again as much.

08

Questions for a staff meeting

Four different questions tend to get flattened into one debate: how much energy a specific task uses, how large a provider's aggregate footprint is, what burden lands on host communities, and whether a given use is worth it educationally. These questions try to keep them separate.

  1. 01A single prompt's footprint is small, but a school's aggregate use adds up to something measurable. Where should a policy actually target — the individual prompt, the tool defaults the school chooses, or something else entirely?
  2. 02Image and video generation cost far more energy per use than text. Does that change which tools you'd pick for which assignments, or is educational value the deciding factor either way?
  3. 03This page corrected several of its own claims after outside review. What would it take for a number already circulating in your building to get the same scrutiny before it's repeated to students?
  4. 04Set the energy question aside for a moment: does a specific classroom use of AI deliver enough educational value to justify whatever its footprint turns out to be, compared to the alternative you'd otherwise use?
